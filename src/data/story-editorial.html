01 / 07SEVENTH GRADE / FOLLOWING A QUESTION

It started with
“What if I try?”

I was a kid with internet access and far too many questions. One answer always led to five more.

I don’t remember sitting down one day and deciding, “I’m going to work in tech.” I was in seventh grade. I had a computer, the internet, and this habit of wondering how things worked. A website looked ordinary to most people. To me, it was a puzzle. Who made the buttons work? Where did the words go when you clicked something? Could I make a page like that myself?

So I started looking things up. I learned the basics of HTML and CSS, then began exploring programming and building little projects of my own. One tutorial led to another. Sometimes I understood what I was doing. Sometimes I copied something, broke it, and spent ages trying to understand why. The best part was the moment it finally clicked.

The internet offered a thousand ways to pass the time. I kept returning to the corner of it that taught me how to make things. My projects were small: simple tools, experiments, things that made a task a bit easier. Most were never meant for anyone else to use. That didn’t bother me. I just liked making something that hadn’t existed on my screen an hour earlier.

I had no idea where any of it would lead. I only knew I wanted to try the next thing.

A few years later, the question wasn’t “Can I make this?” It was “Can I fix something someone needs right now?”

02 / 07AGE 15 / FIRST JOB / PAKISTANI TECH STARTUP

The day I said,
“Let me try.”

The site had gone down. The person who usually fixed these things wasn’t there. I was fifteen—and I asked for a chance.

At fifteen, I was working with a Pakistan-based technology startup. I was learning the unglamorous but important side of websites: keeping them fast, updated, secure, and working when someone needed them.

One day, a WordPress website crashed at the office. The regular developer wasn’t available. People needed the site back, and I could see there was a problem nobody was solving yet.

I went to the CEO and offered to take a look. I didn’t have a ready-made solution. I didn’t know whether it would work, but I wanted to try anyway.

Recreated from memory; not a word-for-word transcript.

He gave me a chance. I started researching, checked what might have gone wrong, tried possible fixes, and kept at it until I helped get the site running again.

I still think about that moment. Not because I performed some impossible technical miracle. I didn’t. I simply asked to help before I knew whether I could. It was the first time that a habit I’d developed as a kid became something another person could count on.

That day taught me to take responsibility. SEO would teach me what responsibility looks like when clients and teams depend on you.

03 / 0729 APRIL 2024 / A UK-BASED MARKETING AGENCY

I came for SEO.
I stayed for the bigger picture.

I thought I was joining a world of keywords and links. I found people, deadlines, trust—and a lot of moving parts.

On April 29, 2024, I joined a UK-based digital marketing agency as an Off-Page SEO Intern. At first, there was plenty to learn: outreach, backlinks, research, and how a campaign actually moves from an idea to a result.

But after a while, I noticed something. The hardest part wasn’t always the SEO. Sometimes it was making sure a client got an answer, a teammate had what they needed, or a task didn’t get lost between two people.

My responsibilities grew. I eventually led client-facing off-page SEO delivery, often handling fifteen or more campaigns at once, sometimes over twenty. I helped train colleagues, improve the process, and build small internal tools when the same repetitive work kept showing up.

That experience changed how I saw the job. Rankings mattered, of course. But so did the person waiting for an update, the teammate who needed support, and the work that had to happen every day for a good plan to mean anything.

I had started by learning how search engines work. I was also learning how people work together.

Once I began seeing the whole problem, not just my part of it, I started saying yes to things outside my job title.

04 / 07US-BASED AGENCY / COORDINATING AT SCALE

Not every problem
came with an SEO label.

Sometimes the useful thing to do had nothing to do with search rankings at all.

I also worked with a US-based digital marketing agency, helping coordinate local SEO for more than twenty clients. It was busy, practical work, and another lesson in what happens when many people are depending on the same team.

At one point, the agency needed video-editing support. I wasn’t a video editor. I didn’t wake up one morning and become one. But I could help find the right people and bring them together. So I helped put a team in place in Pakistan.

That probably sounds like a small detour from SEO. For me, it connected a few dots. The job title wasn’t the interesting part. The interesting part was asking, “What’s actually stopping us?” and figuring out what would make the next step easier.

By then, I was finding the same pattern everywhere: something gets stuck, people work around it for a while, and someone eventually asks whether there’s a better way. I liked being the person who asked.

Then someone asked everyone to build a tool in a single weekend. That question changed the direction of my career.

05 / 07ONE WEEKEND / A SMALL SOFTWARE CHALLENGE

Then came
the weekend challenge.

One invitation to build something useful took me in a direction I hadn’t planned.

The CEO at the UK agency posted a challenge in our company group: build a small tool that solves a real problem over the weekend.

I liked that kind of challenge. No long presentation. No perfect plan. Just find something annoying, make it better, and show what you made. So I gave it a go.

I don’t remember the name of that first tool clearly enough to put it on this page. What I do remember is what happened after I showed the result. The CEO wanted me to work more directly with him, on building useful software.

It felt exciting. It also meant leaving work I knew well. I trained someone to take over my SEO responsibilities and spent roughly a month handing things across. There wasn’t a movie-style moment where I woke up with a whole new career. There were conversations, handovers, questions, and a lot to learn.

But there was that same feeling I’d had years earlier, staring at a screen and wondering if I could make something work. Only this time, the question had opened a door.

The fun part was getting a product to work. The harder part was learning what happens after real people start using it.

06 / 07UK-BASED SOFTWARE PRODUCT COMPANY / HEAD OF SAAS

Launch day was
only the beginning.

Building something is exciting. Keeping it useful when people rely on it is a different kind of challenge.

I moved into a UK-based software product company that builds, launches, and sells its own software internationally. The problems were wonderfully ordinary: scheduling, subscriptions, reporting, search workflows, and all the bits of work that take too much time when nothing connects properly.

I use AI-assisted development tools and software platforms to build and improve products. I’m not going to pretend I hand-code every system from scratch. My strength is starting with the problem, working out what people need, helping build a useful solution, testing it, and sticking around when it needs to change.

Across that work, I’ve contributed to more than fifty software projects. Some were experiments. Some were internal tools. Some never went further. Around twenty or more reached launch. They’re company-owned products, not fifty businesses I started myself.

One product grew out of the headache of managing more than a hundred booking pages. Another came from trying to keep track of recurring subscriptions. That’s the kind of thing I enjoy: a problem people have accepted as normal, until someone says, “Surely this could be easier?”

And launch day? That’s often when the real questions begin. Who helps when a payment fails? What happens when permissions get messy? How do you support users, improve the product, and keep the team moving? I’ve worked on those questions too—from integrations and migrations to launches and everyday operations. I’ve managed teams of more than ten and helped train more than twenty-five people.

The lesson I keep coming back to is simple: a useful product is not just one that works on the day you show it. It’s one that keeps helping people afterward.

The tools changed. The work got bigger. The reason I enjoy it stayed surprisingly familiar.

07 / 07TODAY / NOT THE END OF THE STORY

I’m still the kid
asking questions.

Only now the questions are about products, teams, businesses—and what makes something genuinely useful.

Today, my work lives somewhere between SaaS development, AI automation, and SEO strategy. On paper, those sound like three different careers. To me, they are three ways of asking the same question: what isn’t working, and how can we make it better?

I still want to build a business of my own one day, around software and SEO services. I want to work with people who care about doing thoughtful work, not just making an impressive first impression. But I’m also learning to pay attention to the work right in front of me.

And I have a life outside a screen. I write poetry. I read, cook, and play cricket with friends. Sometimes the best way to come back to a problem is to stop thinking about it for a while.

I don’t have an inspiring speech about knowing exactly where I’ll be in ten years. I don’t. I’m still curious, still learning, still occasionally getting stuck on things that looked simple at first.

But if something breaks—or if there’s a better way to do it—I know what I’ll probably say: “Hey, let me try.”
